Invisalign: how the treatment works

From the digital scan to final retention, what happens at each stage of treatment with clear aligners.

15 January 2026 · 5 min read

People considering orthodontic treatment with clear aligners tend to arrive at the appointment with the same question: how does a plastic tray move a tooth. The answer lies less in the material than in the planning behind it.

The principle: movement in small steps

Each aligner in the sequence is made with a geometry slightly different from the current position of the teeth. That difference applies a light, continuous force, enough to trigger the bone remodelling that allows the tooth to move. The movement planned per aligner is small on purpose: excessive force does not speed treatment up and can cause damage.

The sum of those small steps, across dozens of aligners, is what produces the final result.

Stage 1: diagnosis and scanning

Everything starts with the clinical examination and radiographs. This is where it is decided whether the case is suitable for aligners, whether there is active periodontal disease, and whether there is decay to treat before starting.

Once the indication is confirmed, an intraoral scanner captures the geometry of the arches in three dimensions. The procedure replaces conventional impressions taken with paste.

Stage 2: planning and simulation

With the digital model, the clinician defines the sequence of movements: which teeth move, in what order, in which direction and how far per stage. The software then generates a simulation of the predicted outcome.

That simulation is a projection of the plan, not a guarantee. The actual result depends on the individual biological response and, above all, on sticking to the wear time.

Stage 3: attachments

Some movements, such as rotating rounded teeth or extrusion, need points of purchase. Small composite bumps called attachments are bonded to the tooth surface, giving the aligner somewhere to apply force in the required direction. They are tooth-coloured and are removed at the end of treatment.

Stage 4: daily wear

The recommendation is 20 to 22 hours a day. The aligners are removed to eat, to drink anything other than water, and to brush.

This is the point where treatment depends most on the patient. The change schedule is calculated assuming the recommended wear time; wearing them less does not only cause delay, it can also mean the next aligner no longer fits properly.

Stage 5: follow-up

Follow-up appointments check whether the teeth are tracking the plan. When there is a divergence between what was predicted and what is observed, a new scan can be taken and the remaining sequence reprogrammed.

Stage 6: retention

The bone around the teeth takes time to stabilise in the new position, and the periodontal fibres tend to pull the teeth back. That is why retention is neither optional nor temporary: it is the stage that preserves what was achieved.

It can be fixed, with a wire bonded behind the teeth, removable, with a plate worn at night, or a combination of the two. Which one applies depends on the case.

What to weigh up before deciding

Clear aligners serve a considerable share of cases, but not all of them. Complex movements may respond more predictably to fixed appliances, and the decision has to take that into account honestly.
