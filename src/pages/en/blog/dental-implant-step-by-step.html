The word "implant" tends to focus all the attention on the surgery. In practice, the surgical procedure is a short stage within a process that begins weeks earlier and continues for months afterwards.

What the implant replaces

The implant does not replace the tooth: it replaces the root. It is a titanium post placed in the bone, onto which the prosthesis that plays the part of the crown will be fixed.

That distinction explains why an implant matters even in areas that are barely visible. Without the root, the bone in the region stops receiving mechanical stimulus and is progressively resorbed.

Stage 1: general and oral health assessment

The general picture comes before the local one. Uncontrolled diabetes, smoking, bisphosphonate use and previous radiotherapy in the region all affect healing and have to be taken into account in the plan.

On oral examination, active periodontal disease and sources of infection are treated first. Placing an implant in an environment with active inflammation compromises the result.

Stage 2: the CT scan

A cone beam CT scan shows the volume and density of the available bone and the position of structures that must be preserved, such as the inferior alveolar nerve and the maxillary sinus.

This is the stage that reveals whether there is enough bone or whether grafting will be needed first. A conventional radiograph, being two-dimensional, cannot give that information reliably.

Stage 3: digital planning

With the CT scan and the scan of the arches, the position of each implant is defined in software: depth, angulation and the exact point of entry. Planning starts from where the prosthesis needs to be, not only from where there is bone.

Where the case allows, that plan is converted into a printed surgical guide, which rests on the teeth or the gum and directs the drilling to the planned position.

Stage 4: the surgery

Carried out under local anaesthetic, in an outpatient setting. Placing a single implant usually takes under an hour. With a surgical guide, it is often possible to work with a reduced tissue opening, which tends to lessen post-operative swelling.

There is no pain during the procedure. Post-operative discomfort is comparable to that of an extraction and is controlled with the prescribed medication.

Stage 5: osseointegration

This is the period in which bone grows in direct contact with the titanium surface. It takes three to six months, varying with the region and bone quality.

In selected cases with good primary stability, a temporary prosthesis can be fitted on the day of surgery. That is a possibility rather than the rule, and depends on criteria assessed at the time.

Stage 6: the definitive prosthesis

Once integration is complete, an impression or scan is taken for the definitive prosthesis. It can be screw-retained or cemented onto the implant, and the choice takes into account position, appearance and ease of future maintenance.

Stage 7: maintenance

Implants do not develop decay, but the tissue around them can become inflamed. Peri-implantitis is the main cause of late implant loss, and is associated with plaque accumulation and lack of follow-up.

Good daily hygiene and periodic check-ups are part of the treatment, not an add-on. Read more about dental implants.
